How storms get water into a house

Storm water damage comes from a few repeating paths. Wind driven rain finds failed flashing and aging window seals. A limb or a whole tree opens a roof. Gutters overflow and send water down a foundation wall, and a sump pump that quits during a power outage lets a basement fill.

Each path leaves a different pattern. Roof and window water usually stains ceilings and walls. Foundation water arrives at the floor. In either case the first job is to extract the water, find the source and begin drying before the materials swell and turn.

What Framingham storms have looked like

Framingham's hazard mitigation planning presentation of July 2022 lists nor'easters and other severe winter storms, flooding that includes riverine events, dam failures and ice jams, and other severe weather such as thunderstorms among the hazards the city is studying.

Local news reported the result of one such event. After thunderstorms on July 25, 2023, Boston 25 News described flooded streets, uprooted trees and downed power lines in Framingham, with outages expected to last into the next day. Power loss is the detail that matters most here, because a sump pump without electricity is only a pipe.

The state's ResilientMass plan says annual precipitation in Massachusetts is expected to rise and arrive over fewer days, which it reads as more intense rainfall events. Whatever the trend, a drier home starts with gutters that drain, pumps that work and a plan for outages.

Safety steps before and after the storm passes

Stay away from downed wires and treat every one as live. Do not enter a flooded basement if water is above an outlet, a panel or an appliance. Water that has come from outside can carry contamination, so avoid contact and keep children and pets out.

If a tree or limb has broken the structure, stay out of the damaged area until it is made safe. Call the utility or 911 about electrical, gas or structural hazards before you call us. Work begins once the area is safe and the source is controlled.

What the crew does

The order is the same after every storm, though the details change with the water.

  1. Make the water stop spreading

    Shut off supply lines if a pipe was hit, and set up extraction where water is standing.

  2. Classify the water

    Rain and roof water begin as relatively clean, but ground and flood water is contaminated. The crew decides how to proceed, and flood damage restoration covers contaminated water.

  3. Extract and map

    Pumps and extractors clear standing water, and moisture meters and thermal imaging show what is wet inside walls and ceilings.

  4. Dry to the standard

    Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers run until readings fall to dry levels. ANSI/IICRC S500 is the reference standard behind that approach.

Sump pumps, power outages and basements

A sump pump is only as dependable as its power. If outages are common on your street, a battery backup or a water powered unit gives you a second line of defense. Test the pump before storm season by pouring water into the pit and watching the float rise.

If the pump fails and the basement fills, call for extraction and keep out of the water if power is on. The cause matters for insurance, since coverage for sump pump overflow and seepage often depends on a specific endorsement. Check yours before the next storm.

Insurance after a storm in general terms

Wind and rain damage to the building is handled differently from flood damage. Standard homeowners policies leave out flood, per the Division of Insurance, so FEMA's flood maps and a separate flood policy matter. Ask the insurer which category your loss falls into.

Document the damage from every angle before cleanup, and keep receipts for emergency steps. The Division's Consumer Services Unit at 617-521-7794 can answer general questions.
