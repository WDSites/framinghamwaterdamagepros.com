Why kitchens get hit by water so often

A kitchen holds a sink, a dishwasher, often a refrigerator with an ice maker and several supply lines, all inside cabinets and behind appliances. A small leak is hard to spot because the water goes into places nobody looks.

The city's housing market analysis, which draws on American Community Survey data, lists the 1950s as the most common construction period in Framingham, at about 21 percent of homes. Many kitchens from that era were remodeled over older subfloors, so new flooring and cabinets can sit on layers that stay wet for a long time.

What you see and what it means

Kitchen damage often looks mild on the surface. These signs usually mean more water than it seems.

  • A cabinet door that sticks or a drawer that no longer closes. Swelling has begun.
  • Toe kicks that bubble or peel. Water is sitting at the base of the cabinet.
  • Vinyl or laminate that lifts at the seams. Water has gone beneath it.
  • Hairline cracks or loose grout in tile. The subfloor is moving.
  • A musty smell with no visible puddle. The cavity has been damp for days.

First-hour safety steps in a wet kitchen

Shut off water at the stop valve under the sink, behind the dishwasher or at the main. Kitchen counters carry outlets for appliances, so do not touch anything electrical while standing in water. If water has reached an outlet or the panel, call the utility or 911 before you call us.

If you have a gas range, leave the house and call from outside at any smell of gas. Empty the refrigerator only if water is not near its plug.

Move small items off the counters and floor, pull rugs, and photograph the cabinets, floor and the source before anything is moved. Then call the dispatch line.

How a kitchen is dried

A kitchen is dried from the inside out, because the wet part is usually behind and beneath the cabinets.

  1. Map the wet area

    Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera read the floor, cabinet bases and the walls behind them so nothing is guessed.

  2. Extract and open up

    Standing water is pulled out. Toe kicks come off, and a small section of flooring may be lifted to expose the subfloor.

  3. Dry the cavities

    Air movers push air through the openings and LGR dehumidifiers pull the moisture out of the room.

  4. Check the readings

    The crew logs moisture each visit and adjusts the setup until the cabinets, subfloor and walls match a dry reference.

  5. Plan the rebuild

    What dried is kept. What swelled or stayed wet is removed and replaced through water damage repair.

Flooring, tile and subfloor under the kitchen

Flooring that runs under the cabinets is the hard part. Hardwood and engineered boards can cup when water gets beneath them, vinyl plank can lift at the joints, and tile can loosen if the subfloor swells.

Plywood subfloor dries well when it is reached quickly. If it stays wet it softens and can grow mold, and a section may have to be cut out and replaced. A crew will tell you which areas are in play before any cabinet is moved.

Countertops and backsplash

Countertops rarely need replacing for water that came from below, but the seams and caulk lines can fail if the cabinet beneath swells. Laminate over particleboard is the weakest case. Stone and solid surface tops usually stay, though the cabinets under them may not.

A backsplash that looks fine can hide water behind it. If the wall behind the sink is damp, the tile or board is removed so the cavity can dry.

Insurance, in general terms

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance explains that sudden and accidental water damage is what homeowners policies commonly respond to, with conditions. A dishwasher hose that bursts is sudden. A trap that dripped for months is treated differently.

Flood from outside the home is not the same as a plumbing or appliance leak and is usually excluded from standard policies. Check your policy for how it treats cabinets and flooring, photograph everything and ask your insurer before work starts.

Mistakes to avoid and how to prevent a kitchen leak

A few habits cause most repeat kitchen water damage.

  • Do not leave wet cabinets closed. Open the doors and drawers so air reaches the cavity.
  • Do not install new flooring over a subfloor that has not been read with a moisture meter.
  • Do not ignore a small drip under the sink. Put a bowl under it and check it every day until it is repaired.
  • Replace old rubber lines with braided lines and keep the area under the sink clear so a leak is seen early.

Permits and local rules in Framingham

A kitchen rebuild often touches plumbing, electrical or structure. Massachusetts plumbing rules in 248 CMR require a permit for most plumbing work, with an exemption for repairing a leak in a faucet, valve or working fixture part. The Framingham Inspectional Services Division at 508-532-5500 can tell you what applies to the rest.

We dry to the ANSI/IICRC S500 reference standard and coordinate the rebuild with the trades who hold the permits.
