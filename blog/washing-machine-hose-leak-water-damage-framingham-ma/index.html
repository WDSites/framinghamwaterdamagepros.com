Washing Machine Hose Leak Water Damage in Framingham, MA

A failed washer hose can put a lot of water on a floor in a short time. Here is how to stop it, dry it and decide what happens next.

By the Framingham Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026-10-29.

(508) 446-9244All services

Stop the water first, then think

If a washing machine hose has burst or popped off, close the shutoff valves behind the machine. If you cannot reach them, shut off the main water valve for the house. Unplug the washer only if you can do it without standing in water. If the outlet is wet, leave it alone and cut power at the panel only when you can reach it dry.

Once the water stops, lift anything off the floor that can be lifted. Towels and a wet vacuum are fine for a clean-water spill on a hard floor, but they do not reach water that has run into the subfloor or down through the ceiling below.

Take photographs before you start cleaning. The hose, the shutoff valves, the wet floor and any damaged walls all help if you make an insurance claim.

What kind of water is it?

Framingham's water comes through the city Department of Public Works distribution system and is purchased from the Massachusetts Water Resources Authority. A burst supply hose therefore starts as clean Category 1 water under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard.

The important word is starts. Water that soaks into carpet, pad or drywall for a day or two can pick up contaminants and move to Category 2. Discharge from the machine's drain hose is different from the beginning. Water that has been through a wash cycle is generally treated as Category 2, because it carries detergent, soil and bacteria.

Knowing which hose failed helps the crew choose between drying in place and removing material.

Supply hose or drain hose: why it matters

Washers have two kinds of hoses and they fail in different ways.

What a crew does after a washer leak

Laundry rooms are often near basements or on upper floors, so the first question is where the water went.

  1. Trace the water

    Technicians check the floor, walls, the ceiling below and cabinets nearby. Water follows pipes, joists and gaps and often travels farther than the visible puddle.

  2. Extract and lift

    Standing water is removed. Carpet or vinyl is lifted if water is trapped under it, and baseboards are opened so walls can dry.

  3. Dry with equipment

    Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed to dry the floor system and the wall cavities. Readings are logged until they reach a dry standard.

  4. Decide on removal

    Wet drywall, insulation and flooring are removed only if they cannot be dried safely. The crew shows you readings and photographs.

  5. Hand off to repair

    When the structure is dry, repair work replaces what was removed. See below for how that phase is handled.

Common places the water hides

After a washer leak, the damage is rarely limited to the room you can see. Water often runs under vinyl and laminate to the walls. In a second-floor laundry, it can come through the ceiling below and into light fixtures or a hallway.

In basements, it can flow toward a floor drain or settle under finished flooring. Behind the washer, drywall wicks water upward, and cabinets next to the machine soak up water through their bases.

If you see ceiling staining, bubbling paint or warped baseboards a day or two after a leak, water is still in the structure. A moisture meter and thermal camera can locate it without cutting open every wall.

Document the loss before cleanup

Insurance questions are easier when the evidence is clear.

What to photograph and keep

  • ✓The failed hose and the shutoff valves, before anyone touches them.
  • ✓The wet floor and the water line on walls, cabinets and the washer itself.
  • ✓The ceiling and rooms directly below the laundry area.
  • ✓Damaged belongings, with a simple list of what they were and roughly when you bought them.
  • ✓The date and time you noticed the leak and when you shut off the water.
  • ✓Receipts for supplies, towels or anything else you buy to limit the damage.

Your insurer will tell you what documentation it needs. Ask whether it needs to see the damage before materials are removed.

Insurance in plain terms

Many homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water damage, which is how a burst hose is usually described. Damage from a slow leak that went unnoticed for weeks is treated differently, and many policies exclude it. The cause and the timing both matter.

Coverage, deductibles and what counts as maintenance are set by your policy. Call your insurer, tell them what happened and ask what they need. Keep photographs and receipts together.

Framingham permits: replacing lines, valves and the machine hookup

The City of Framingham Inspectional Services Division handles plumbing and gas permits and inspections as well as building permits. If a repair goes beyond swapping a hose and involves replacing a shutoff valve, a drain line or other plumbing, ask whether a permit is needed before work begins.

The division's main number is 508-532-5500. Permit rules vary with the scope of the work, so a quick call settles what applies and who must pull it. A licensed plumber will usually know the answer, but you can confirm it yourself.

Doing this before a repair keeps the rebuild moving. Missed permits can slow a sale or an insurance sign-off later.

How to avoid the next leak

Check the hoses behind the washer on a schedule. Look for bulges, cracks and rust at the fittings. Many people replace rubber hoses with braided stainless steel ones, and the machine's manual or the hose maker's guidance tells you how often to inspect and replace them.

Turn the washer's shutoff valves off when you are away for several days. Leave a few inches of space behind the machine so you can see the hoses, and keep a drain pan or leak sensor in laundry rooms above finished space.
