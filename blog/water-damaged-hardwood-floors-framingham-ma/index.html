Water Damaged Hardwood Floors in Framingham, MA: Save or Replace?

Hardwood can sometimes be saved if water is removed and the floor is dried fast. This guide explains the signs, the options and when replacement is the better call.

By the Framingham Water Damage Pros team. Published 2026-10-29.

(508) 446-9244All services

Can water damaged hardwood floors be saved?

Sometimes, yes. Hardwood that got wet from a clean source, such as a supply line or a short appliance leak, can often be dried in place if the water is removed quickly and the boards and subfloor are dried with controlled airflow. Hardwood that sat in water for days, or was touched by sewage or flood water, usually has to come up.

Wood absorbs water slowly and releases it slowly. That is why a floor can look fine for a day and then begin to cup, with edges rising higher than the centers of the boards, or crown, with the centers rising instead. Both are signs the wood took on more moisture than it can shed on its own.

The first 48 hours decide most of the outcome. If water is on the floor now, remove what you can, keep people off the boards and call for drying help.

A crew confirms each of these with a moisture meter. Appearance alone does not tell you how wet the wood is.

What to do in the first 48 hours

  1. Stop the source and remove standing water

    Shut off the supply, mop or pump up standing water, and lift rugs and furniture legs off the boards. Do not use a regular household vacuum on wet floors.

  2. Keep foot traffic off

    Wet boards are soft and can dent or lift. Keep children, pets and shoes away until the floor is assessed.

  3. Get air and dehumidification moving

    Household fans alone are rarely enough. A dehumidifier in a closed room helps, and a crew can use specialized mats and panels that pull water from beneath the boards.

  4. Have the subfloor and the cavity checked

    Water often travels under the boards. A moisture meter and a thermal camera can find wet areas you cannot see.

  5. Hold off on sanding and refinishing

    Refinishing wet or unevenly dry wood traps moisture and can ruin the floor. Wait for final readings.

Drying in place versus replacing boards

Drying in place, which falls under water mitigation, is gentler on your budget and your home. It works best when the water was clean, the response was fast and the subfloor can be reached. Specialized drying systems can pull moisture from beneath hardwood, though not every floor responds equally.

Replacement is the better call when boards are buckled, when mold is present or when the water was contaminated. Replacing a section can be cleaner than waiting weeks and then discovering the floor never flattened. If only part of the room is affected, ask about blending new boards into existing ones and how the finish will match.

Engineered hardwood and solid hardwood react differently, and floors installed over concrete or radiant heat add variables. A crew with a moisture meter can tell you more than any rule of thumb.

Questions to ask before anyone sands or refinishes

Refinishing is the final step, not the first. Before a floor is sanded, ask for moisture readings from the boards and the subfloor, and compare them to readings from an unaffected area of the same floor. If the readings match, the floor is ready. If not, wait.

Ask also about the plan if boards gap or cup after drying. Sometimes a floor settles back as it equalizes, and sometimes it does not. A good contractor will describe both outcomes and what each would cost in broad terms, without quoting a number before seeing the floor.

Rebuilding the floor is part of water damage repair, and it comes after the structure is dry. Starting repair early risks sealing moisture in.

Engineered wood, concrete slabs and radiant heat

Not every wood floor behaves like traditional solid planks. Engineered hardwood has layers that can swell and delaminate when wet, and it is often harder to repair than solid wood. Floors installed directly over concrete can trap moisture between the slab and the boards.

Radiant heat adds another variable. Heating a wet floor to speed drying can make boards shrink and gap, so follow the crew's guidance rather than turning up the system. Floating floors can sometimes be lifted, dried and reinstalled, while glued-down floors usually cannot.

Tell the crew what kind of floor you have and how it was installed. That detail changes the plan.

Flood-prone areas and finished floors in Framingham

A federal grant of $75,000 funded a study of flooding in neighborhoods along Walnut Street, and in 2021 the state awarded $269,030 for design and permitting of work to increase the capacity of Sucker Brook in the same area.

The practical point for a homeowner with hardwood is that finished floors in low-lying areas deserve extra care when heavy rain is forecast. If you live near a brook or pond, check your address on the FEMA flood map, keep valuables and rugs off the floor in a storm and know who you will call. Flood water is treated as contaminated, and hardwood touched by it is rarely salvageable.
