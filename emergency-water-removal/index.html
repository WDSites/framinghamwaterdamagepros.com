When standing water is an emergency

Water you can see on the floor is the easy part to remove and the part that does the most harm if it waits. Our dispatch line answers 24/7, and a crew with pumps and extraction units is sent as soon as one is available, with same-day response as the aim. While you wait, there are a few steps worth taking.

Stop the source if you can reach it. That usually means the main shut-off valve near where the water line enters the house. Move anything valuable above the waterline, and keep everyone out of the water if any appliance, cord or outlet is touching it.

If the water is rising from a floor drain, a toilet or a sump pit during a storm, do not wade in. Water that has come from outside or from a sewer line can carry contamination, and our flood damage restoration crews treat it differently from a clean supply-line leak.

Safety first: electricity and water

If water has reached the electrical panel, outlets or anything plugged in, stay out of the wet area. Shut off power at the panel only if you can reach it standing on dry ground. Call the utility or 911 for any electrical or gas concern before you call a restoration crew, because pumps cannot go in until the room is safe.

How standing water is actually pulled out

The tool depends on the depth. A few inches across a finished basement call for submersible pumps that sit in the water and push it out through hoses. Shallower water on hard floors and carpet is removed with extraction units, which are wet vacuums built to lift water out of fibers and pad.

Truck-mounted extractors move the most water in the least time, and portable units reach rooms a truck hose cannot. Crews often use both on the same job, starting with the pump and finishing with extraction so the carpet and pad are not left holding gallons.

Removing the water is step one of three. Moisture stays in the subfloor, wall bottoms and framing after the visible water is gone, so the next stop is drying under water mitigation. Skipping that phase is how a floor that looks fine turns into a musty one two weeks later.

Burst pipe water damage: find it, stop it, then remove the water

A burst pipe is the classic after-hours call. Water pours from a pipe inside a wall, a ceiling cavity or a basement joist bay, and it can put hundreds of gallons into a house before anyone notices. The first job is the shut-off valve, then the water that has already spread.

In Massachusetts winters the cause is often a frozen section of pipe on an exterior wall or an unheated space. The Massachusetts Division of Insurance notes that most homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental pipe bursts when the home was properly heated and occupied, so keep the heat on and photograph the damage before anything is moved.

Crews trace the break by following the wet path with a moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera, since the visible stain is often several feet from the real leak. A plumber repairs the pipe. The restoration crew handles everything the water touched.

Crawl spaces, basements and whole-house water

Crawl spaces and basements hold water longest because there is nowhere for it to drain. A few inches in a Framingham basement can soak the bottom of every wall and the stored items against them, and crawl space water can sit unseen under a floor for days.

Large losses, such as a whole floor under water, get a bigger equipment list and more than one crew visit. If a storm flooded the neighborhood and the water came from outside, the job also moves into flood territory, where insurance rules change and a separate flood policy matters.
