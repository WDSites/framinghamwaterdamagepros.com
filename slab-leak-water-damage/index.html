Slab leaks are uncommon here, and that changes how to read the problem

A slab leak is a leak in a water line that runs under or inside a concrete floor slab. In warmer parts of the country it is a routine call. In Framingham it is not, because a typical house sits over a basement or a crawl space, and the supply lines run through open joist bays where a leak shows itself quickly.

That is worth knowing before you pay for the wrong fix. If you have a basement and water is on the floor, the cause is far more likely a pipe, a sump failure or groundwater than a line buried in concrete. In that case the pages on emergency water removal and flood damage restoration describe the common situation better than this one.

This page is for the homes where a slab really is the floor. Those homes exist here, and when a line under the concrete fails, the water has nowhere obvious to go.

Signs that water is escaping under your floor

Slab leaks are quiet at first. The slow ones show up as a water bill that climbs with no change in how you live. Check the meter at a time when every tap, toilet and appliance is off. If the dial keeps turning, water is leaving the house somewhere.

Other signs are physical. A section of floor that feels warm may sit over a hot water line. Laminate or hardwood may cup or lift. Carpet can feel damp along baseboards, and a sour smell can come from the pad. A faint hiss or running sound in a silent house often means pressurized water is escaping.

None of these proves a slab leak, because a wet wall or a failing toilet seal can produce the same clues. They do mean it is time to stop guessing and shut the water down at the main.

Safety and first-hour steps for a suspected slab leak

Shut off the main water valve where the line enters the house. Turn off the water heater, and if you have radiant heat, turn off the boiler so it stops refilling the loops. Keep electronics, cords and power strips off the damp floor. If water is near an outlet or the panel, stay out and call the utility or 911 before anyone else. Then call a plumber for the line and call us for the water.

How the damage is dried once the line is found

The order is fixed by the situation. Nobody can dry a floor that is still being fed, so the plumber comes first and we follow.

  1. The plumber finds and fixes the line

    Plumbing work in Massachusetts generally needs a permit issued to a licensed plumber, under the state plumbing regulation 248 CMR. The rules allow an emergency repair before the permit, as long as the inspector is told and the work is inspected by the next working day.

  2. We map where the water went

    Moisture meters and a thermal imaging camera show how far the water traveled under flooring and along the bottom of walls. Concrete slabs hold moisture and give it back slowly, so a dry surface does not mean a dry slab.

  3. Extract and open up

    Standing water is removed with extraction units. Wet carpet pad is lifted, and baseboards may be pulled so the wall bottoms can breathe.

  4. Dry with air movers and LGR dehumidifiers

    Air movers sweep the slab surface and low-grain refrigerant dehumidifiers pull moisture out of the air. Floating floors may be lifted in sections so air reaches the concrete.

  5. Check and log until dry

    Technicians record readings each visit. ANSI/IICRC S500 is the published reference standard for this kind of drying, and it expects measured results rather than a set number of days.

Water coming up through a basement slab is a different cause

If your floor is a basement slab and water is seeping through cracks or along the wall joint, the source is most likely outside the house, not a pipe. The Framingham hazard mitigation plan, updated in 2023 and approved by FEMA, records that many cellars in the city flood from groundwater, and that the Fire Department lends sump pumps to homeowners.

That kind of water is not a slab leak, and the plumber you call for a buried line cannot fix it. It is handled as standing water first and as a drying job after, and a separate flood policy may matter. Start with emergency water removal if water is on the floor now.

A quick test helps. Tape a square of plastic to a dry patch of floor and leave it for a day. Moisture under the plastic points to vapor or seepage from below, and a wet top surface points to a leak above.

How insurance tends to treat a leak under the slab

Coverage depends on the policy and on how the leak is described. Sudden and accidental water damage is often covered, and a leak that went on slowly over a long time often is not. Some policies treat the cost of reaching a buried pipe differently from the damage the water caused.

The Massachusetts Division of Insurance advises photographing the damage before temporary repairs and keeping receipts for them. It also says to call your agent or the claims line as soon as you can and to read what the policy says about exclusions.

Census Bureau figures put the owner-occupied rate in Framingham at 54.8 percent, so many households are renters or condo owners who answer to a landlord or an association. If that is you, tell the property manager at once and ask who handles the plumber and who handles the floor.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not drill or cut into the slab yourself to look for a pipe. A pressurized line can spray, and cutting concrete throws up dust and can damage other lines or radiant tubing. Leak detection is a trade with its own tools, so let it go to a plumber.

Do not lay new flooring over a floor that has been wet. Sealed-in moisture is how adhesive fails and boards lift. And do not wait for a bill to prove it, because the longer water travels, the more wall and cabinet material it reaches.
